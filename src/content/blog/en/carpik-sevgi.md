"I'm only telling you because I love you."

What did you feel when you read that sentence? Perhaps a warmth. Perhaps a tightening. Perhaps both at once.

One of the most unsettling discoveries in learning Nonviolent Communication is this: a great deal of what we say and do in the name of love produces fear, guilt or shame in the person receiving it. Which is to say, technically, it contains violence.

In this piece I want to talk about the pattern I call "distorted love" — why it forms, how it looks, and how we might find our way out of it.

## Where conditional love leaves its mark

Most of us grew up with conditional love. That is not an accusation but an observation, because most of our parents grew up with it too.

The essence of conditional love is this: be "good" and you will be loved. Be "good enough" and you will be approved of. Make a mistake — or fail to meet certain expectations — and what follows is withdrawal, coldness, expressions of disappointment.

As children we take the message in: *love is earned. And if I try hard enough, it means I am good enough.*

That template travels into adulthood. And now we begin sending the same message to the people we love — most of the time without noticing.

## The three faces of distorted love

### 1. Criticism that arrives as "because I love you"

The pattern sounds like this:

- "Because I love you — you're putting on weight, and I have to say it."
- "Because I want what's best for you, that friend isn't good for you."
- "Because I worry about you, this decision is a mistake."

Is there real love here? Very probably, yes. But love is being used as an *instrument* — to soften the fact that what is being said is criticism.

Seen through NVC, sentences like these usually reflect the speaker's own unmet needs: a need for safety, a need for control, or a need to make their own criticism legitimate.

### 2. Demands for love that arrive through the victim role

"After all I've sacrificed, is this all I'm worth?"

"It upsets me so much when you behave like that" — this is not an expression of a need; it is usually a message loaded with guilt.

These patterns cover over the speaker's real need — to be seen, to be valued, to be connected — and leave the receiver busy managing guilt. They create a relationship of debt rather than of connection.

### 3. Protection as control

This is the form most often applied to children and young people:

- Forbidding certain clothes "for their own good"
- Blocking certain friendships "so they don't get hurt"
- Restricting choices "so they won't regret it"

There is genuine love here, and a genuine need to protect. But the method damages the person's autonomy and their confidence in themselves. The message is: *I cannot trust your decisions. I know better.*

## Why is it so common?

Because we were taught it. Because language is learned — and most of us grew up surrounded by people speaking this one.

And there is something else: we really are worried. We really do love. It is only that our means of expressing that worry and that love are narrow.

Marshall Rosenberg put it this way: violent communication is communication disconnected from the heart. The problem is not an absence of love, but a broken connection to the heart.

## Is another way possible?

Yes. And it is both harder and far more powerful.

Instead of "I'm only telling you because I love you":

*"I'm worried about you, and I want to share that worry with you. But the worry is mine — the decision is yours."*

Did you feel the difference? In the second, the worry is real and the person's autonomy is respected.

Or, instead of the victim pattern:

*"I've been feeling unseen for a while. I'd like to spend more time with you."*

That is a direct and undefended expression of a need. It carries no guilt. It is an invitation into connection.

## A question to practise with

This week, when you want to say something to someone you love, ask yourself:

*What does this sentence want to stir in the other person? Love — or debt, guilt, or fear?*

The answer will not always be clear. But asking the question is a transformation in itself.
